Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions winding shops ask most often.

What happens if I change the number of turns per coil?

Turns set the magnetic flux. Fewer turns than the original design raises the flux density, so the motor draws more no-load current and runs hot. More turns lowers the flux and the torque. Small changes (±5%) are usually tolerated, but a rewind should match the original turns unless you are deliberately redesigning the motor for a different voltage.

Can I rewind a copper motor with aluminium wire?

Yes, but not with the same diameter. Aluminium conducts only about 61% as well as copper, so you need roughly 1.28 times the cross-section (about 1.13 times the diameter) for the same resistance, which is hard to fit in the same slots. Copper remains the standard choice for rewinds where slot space is tight.

From what size should I use star-delta starting?

As a rule of thumb, from about 11 kW (15 HP) upwards, or whenever direct starting would dip the supply voltage enough to disturb other equipment. The exact point depends on your transformer size and supply impedance — check the voltage drop at start with the calculator above before deciding.

How do I size a run capacitor for a single-phase motor?

A common starting point is about 70 µF per kW at 230 V / 50 Hz, then fine-tune by measuring the auxiliary winding current — the correct capacitor makes the main and auxiliary winding currents roughly equal under load. Use the capacitor calculator above for the estimate, and always use a capacitor rated for continuous AC motor duty at the right voltage.

Why does voltage drop matter for motors?

Motor torque falls with the square of the voltage, so a 10% voltage drop costs about 19% of starting torque — enough to stall a loaded pump or compressor at start. Long cable runs also waste energy as heat. Keeping the drop within 5% protects starting performance and efficiency.

What slot fill factor should I aim for in a rewind?

Keep the slot fill factor roughly between 35% and 55%. Below that range you are wasting slot space and copper performance; above it the coils become very hard to insert without damaging the enamel insulation, and heat has less room to escape.

How do I convert HP to kW for the calculators?

Multiply horsepower by 0.746: for example 5 HP ≈ 3.7 kW and 10 HP ≈ 7.5 kW. The reference table above lists both kW and HP for the most common motor sizes.
